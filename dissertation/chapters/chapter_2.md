## 2.1 Preamble

This chapter provides a review of theoretical and empirical literature relevant to understanding how Pension Fund Administrators (PFAs) employ heuristics in the selection of their property portfolios. It considers available literary evidence to assess whether the use of heuristics in property portfolio selection represents adaptive responses to environmental constraints, or if they are simply systematic biases that cause investment managers to lose money.

This review begins by exploring the characteristics of real estate as an investment asset class and within the lens of the Nigerian property market before moving on to discuss the concept of portfolio construction and diversification. It then moves on to discuss property selection decision criteria for portfolios before settling on the concept, types and manifestations of heuristic in real estate portfolios. The chapter concludes with a review of theories relevant to the research, a chronological review of relevant existing literature, and a framework that conceptualizes the entire chapter and forms the basis for subsequent chapters.

## 2.2 Property Investment as an Asset Class

Real estate or Real Property possesses distinctive physical, financial, and market characteristics that fundamentally differentiate it from traditional investment securities such as stocks and bonds. These unique features both create opportunities and pose challenges that influence institutional investment decision-making, portfolio construction and diversification methods (Geltner et al., 2014; Hoesli & Lizieri, 2007).

### 2.2.1 Physical Characteristics of Real Estate

The physical characteristics of real estate impose certain constraints absent in other financial securities. These features are fundamental and affect real estate irrespective of location or any other conditions. They include:

i. **Indivisibility:** This refers to the fact that properties cannot be traditionally purchased in fractional units. An investor cannot acquire 5% of an office building in the way one might purchase 100 shares representing 5% ownership of a corporation (Baum & Hartzell, 2012).

ii. **Heterogeneity:** The uniqueness of each property means that no two assets are ever truly identical even within the same class and location (DiPasquale & Wheaton, 1996).

iii. **Immobility:** Permanence of location creates a situation where value depends critically on neighborhood characteristics, accessibility, zoning, and environmental factors specific to the property's geographical co-ordinates (Thrall, 2002).

### 2.2.2 Financial Characteristics of Real Estate

Beyond physical attributes, real estate exhibits certain financial characteristics that define its role in investment portfolios. These attributes can either be positive or negative. Investment and

Portfolio managers must have a good knowledge of these characteristics and their macro effect on portfolio performance. They include:

i. **Income generation:** This typically occurs through rental income, and provides relatively stable and predictable cash flow, particularly for institutional-grade properties with credit-worthy tenants on long-term leases (Baum, 2015).

ii. **Appreciation potential:** This arises from both physical improvements to the land or building, and market dynamics. Properties in growth markets may appreciate substantially as neighbourhoods develop, transportation infrastructure improves, or zoning changes permit higher-density use (DiPasquale & Wheaton, 1996).

iii. **Inflation-hedging:** This is a unique property of real estate that represents a particularly valuable characteristic for long-term investors like pension funds. Real estate values and rental income have historically exhibited positive correlation with inflation, as rising general price levels increase construction costs (supporting property values) and enable rent escalation (Fama & Schwert, 1977; Brueggeman et al., 1984; Nwosu et al., 2024).

iv. **Illiquidity and high transaction costs:** This constitutes the most significant financial challenge of real estate investment. Real estate transactions require time for marketing, due diligence, negotiation, and legal documentation (Ling, 2005). Transaction costs, including brokerage commissions, legal fees, valuation fees, stamp duties, and other charges, creates substantial friction that discourages frequent trading (Ling & Archer, 2020; Aluko, 2007).

### 2.2.3 Real Estate in Institutional Portfolios

Having already established the specific physical and financial characteristics of real estate, it is important to examine the role of real estate in institutional portfolios. Institutional investors include real estate in their portfolios for four primary reasons: return enhancement, diversification of risk, income stability and protection from inflation (Baum & Hertzel, 2012; Geltner et al., 2014).

Among the primary reasons for the inclusion of real estate in institutional portfolios, the opportunity for diversification of risk represents the most important and interesting reason. In line with the diversification framework of Markowitz (1952), the addition of real estate to a mixed-asset portfolio can reduce total portfolio risk without proportionally reducing expected returns (Hartzel et al., 1986).

While the addition of real estate can effect positive results on institutional portfolios, that may not always be the case. Research by Okeahialam & Kalu (2024) studying the performance of real estate instruments in the investing of pension funds in Nigeria from 2012 - 2021 found that returns from direct real estate properties declined steadily, dropping from 58% in 2012 to 18.16% in 2021. Quarterly Pencom Reports (Q3, 2025) also show that the allocation percentage of PFAs to direct real estate sits at 0.93%, significantly lower than the regulatory allowance of 10%. From all available evidence, real estate within the context of institutional portfolios in Nigeria is on a downward trajectory both in terms of allocation and performance. The next section analyzes distinctive features of the Nigerian property market that may be resulting in this significant institutional real estate under-allocation and portfolio under-performance.

### 2.2.4 Distinctive Features of Nigerian Real Estate Markets

While the characteristics described in Section 2.2 above apply generally to real estate as an asset class, the Nigerian property market exhibits several distinctive features that could amplify both opportunities and challenges for institutional investors. They are as follows:

i. **Title documentation complexity:** The Land Use Act of 1978 vested all land in each state in the governor holding it in trust for the people (Land Use Act, 1978). This means that “ownership of land” is merely a right of occupancy as opposed to actual freehold ownership, which is evident in the Certificate of Occupancy (C of O). Transferring this right requires “Governor's Consent”, which adds an extra layer of complexity and bureaucracy (Aluko, 2007). Furthermore, as explained by Smith (2013), other forms of title documentation such as Gazette, Excision, and Deed of Assignment exist within the Nigerian regulatory framework. This naturally creates a hierarchy of title quality (C of O > Governor’s Consent > Gazette > Excision > Deed of Assignment) with C of O being the most credible, and Deed of Assignment being the least credible.

ii. **Market opacity and information asymmetry:** Nigeria lacks a reliable property transaction record system. Most transactions are privately conducted, with prices remaining undisclosed, leading to situations where buyers and sellers enter into negotiations with vastly different levels of information, mostly skewed in favour of the sellers (Olapade et al., 2019). This creates severe information asymmetry and is well documented as Nigeria ranks within the “Low transparency” tiers of the Global Real Estate Transparency Index (JLL, 2024). Resultantly, institutional investors may have to rely on subjective judgements and assumptions, rather than reliable data.

iii. **High transaction costs:** While high costs of transaction is a primary feature of real estate globally, transaction costs in Nigeria exceed those in developed markets. The NIESV’s scale for agency and valuation fees (2014) sets these values at about 5 - 10%. Aluko (2007) argues that once these costs accumulate (combined with other taxes and levies), they create the constrained, illiquid real estate market that we know. In light of the above, it can be deduced that the cost of acquiring or disposing of real estate represents a good percentage of its capital value. As a result of this, short-term portfolio rebalancing becomes much less viable and less attractive of a prospect to institutional investors, leading them to either invest substantial amounts of capital into “lumpy”, illiquid assets, or invest said capital in more liquid markets.

In light of the above unique features of the Nigerian property market, the next chapter discusses the specifics of property portfolio construction and how it relates to emerging, data-scarce property markets like Nigeria’s.

## 2.3 Property Portfolio Selection

The fundamentals of property portfolio selection is rooted in Markowitz (1952, 1959)’s Modern Portfolio Theory, which posits that investors can optimize their portfolio returns while simultaneously minimizing unsystematic risk through the diversification of assets. Investors can achieve this by combining assets with low correlation in order to safely absorb risk and boost overall returns. Aside from Modern Portfolio Theory techniques, Olaleye (2011) discusses extensively the naïve diversification strategies that apply conventional wisdom and rule of thumb in the selection of portfolio assets and diversification strategies. This section expounds on the principles behind property portfolio diversification and the inherent strategies.

### 2.3.1 Principles of Portfolio Diversification

Olaleye (2011) defines Portfolio diversification as the technique of varying investment possibilities in order to minimise the encompassed risks and maximise the return therefrom. American economist, Harry Markowitz, also won the Nobel prize in economics in 1990 for his research work on Portfolio theory in the 1950s. The crux of his theory centred on the idea that the combination of assets with less than positive correlation serves to reduce the weighted averaged variance (risk) of the portfolio beyond the level of risk associated with individual constituents.

Having established the concept of diversification and its relative importance in portfolio selection, it is important to assess if the extent of diversification of a portfolio can be measured. Choueifaty & Coignard (2008) introduced the Diversification Ratio (DR) as a metric for capturing portfolio selection quality where the weighted average of individual asset volatilities is divided by portfolio volatility.

When assets are perfectly correlated, the ratio equals 1, signaling that no diversification benefit is achieved. As correlations decrease, the diversification ratio rises above 1 with higher values indicating more effective risk reduction through diversification.

From the above evidence, the guiding principles behind portfolio diversification can be deduced as the need for investors to maximize their profits while simultaneously minimizing their risk. Olaleye (2011) proceeds to state that there are two main approaches to portfolio diversification, namely: the traditional approach or naïve diversification and the Modern Portfolio Theory - based approach. The rest of this sub-section takes a cursory look at these approaches and their suitability for institutional investors like PFAs.

### 2.3.2 Naïve Diversification Strategies

Naïve or traditional diversification strategies involve the use of conventional wisdom and intuition in the portfolio selection process. This description is supported in literature, first by Hadaway (1978), and subsequently by Hargitay and Yu (1993). Within the context of real estate, naïve diversification has majorly been explored along two dimensions: geographic/economic diversification and property type diversification.

Geographic diversification is based on the idea that real estate investments, as a function of location, vary in terms or returns and risk. As such, investors would need to consider the challenges and opportunities present at various locations and select properties accordingly (Olaleye, 2011).

Property type diversification has its roots in the idea that the returns and risks associated with a property varies according to the particular industries utilizing said property. Different types of properties respond to the needs of different sectors, and for investors, this means being aware of the factors affecting the users of each property type and their inherent characteristics (Olaleye, 2011). This diversification strategy involves investing in various property types like office buildings, warehouses and hotels (Penny, 1982).

Research from scholars such as Del Casino (1995) and Olaleye & Aluko (2007) has uncovered other strategies that have garnered investor attention in recent years, namely: managers diversification, lease diversification, timing diversification, investment structure diversification, and investment vehicle diversification.

Different studies have tested the efficacy of naïve diversification strategies, most notably Olaleye et al. (2008) and DeMiguel et al. (2009). Their findings point to the idea that naïve diversification proves to be rather competitive, sometimes even outperforming quantitative analysis in out-of-sample situations. Consistent results like these only serve to reinforce the applicability of naïve diversification and justify its position in this study as a valid strategy for portfolio construction.

### 2.3.3 Modern Portfolio Theory: Foundations and Application to Real Estate

This subsection shifts the focus away from naïve diversification strategies to diversification as prescribed by the Modern Portfolio Theory. It examines the foundations of the theory, its guiding principles, and its application to real estate.

### 2.3.3.1 Foundations of Modern Portfolio Theory

Modern Portfolio Theory (MPT), originating with Markowitz's (1952) article "Portfolio
Selection," revolutionized investment analysis by formalizing how investors should construct
portfolios to optimize the tradeoff between expected return and risk. This theory has become the dominant framework in institutional asset management, as it provides the theoretical foundation for asset allocation, performance evaluation, and portfolio optimization across virtually all asset classes including real estate (Elton et al., 2014; Reilly & Brown, 2012).

Modern Portfolio Theory is founded on the mean-variance framework that assumes that investors prefer higher returns (mean) and lower risk (variance). Expected return for a portfolio of assets is therefore the weighted average of individual asset returns (Elton et al., 2014).

Following through with the mean-variance framework, for any set of assets characterized by expected returns, variances, and covariances, there exists a set of portfolios that are mean-variance efficient. These portfolios offer the maximum expected return for a given level of risk, or equivalently, the minimum risk for a target expected return (Markowitz, 1952). The collection of all efficient portfolios forms the efficient frontier, typically depicted as a curve in mean-variance space (Elton et al., 2014).

Portfolios below the efficient frontier are ‘dominated’, meaning that there exist alternative combinations offering either higher return for the same risk or lower risk for the same return. As later on stated by Merton (1972), rational investors should hold only efficient portfolios. His argument was that holding dominated portfolios implies either irrationality or constraints preventing access to efficient allocations.

Constructing the efficient frontier requires solving an optimization problem. In Markowitz's original formulation in 1952, the investor minimizes portfolio variance for a specified target return. This optimization problem is solved systematically by varying a target return (R*) and by solving for minimum variance at each target, the efficient frontier is traced out.

### 2.3.3.2 Applications of Modern Portfolio Theory to Real Estate

Among the earliest applications of portfolio theory to real estate was Friedman's (1971) analysis of REIT diversification, which demonstrated that combining properties across geographic markets and property types could reduce portfolio variance, empirically validating Markowitz's diversification principles within a real estate context. Robichek, Cohn, and Pringle (1972) extended this work by explicitly formulating a mean-variance optimization model for real estate portfolios, showing that efficient frontiers could be constructed using property-level return and risk data.

Throughout the 1970s-1990s, research progressively refined real estate portfolio optimization, examining diversification benefits across property types (office, retail, industrial, residential) and geographic markets (Hartzell, Hekman & Miles, 1986; Malizia, 1991). These studies generally confirmed that real estate exhibits positive risk-return tradeoffs and that diversification across property types and locations improves portfolio efficiency, though the magnitude of benefits and optimal allocations remained subjects of debate (Giliberto, 1993; Mueller, 1993).

### 2.3.3.3 Real Estate-Specific Challenges in Portfolio Optimization

Applying Modern Portfolio Theory to real estate encounters several distinctive challenges absent
in equity portfolio optimization:

i. **Data scarcity in emerging markets:** Constructing efficient frontiers requires expected returns, variances, and a full covariance matrix across all potential properties. In developing countries like Nigeria, transaction data is sparse, inconsistently reported, and often unreliable, making parameter estimation highly uncertain (Olapade et al., 2019; Aluko, 2007).

ii. **Return estimation difficulties:** Real estate returns comprise rental income and capital appreciation. While rental income is observable from lease contracts, capital appreciation requires either appraisals (which are infrequent and potentially biased) or transaction prices (which are rare for individual properties) (Babawale & Ajayi, 2011; Geltner, 1991).

iii. **Covariance matrix construction:** The covariance matrix for assets requires unique covariances. For a portfolio of 50 properties, this entails 1,225 covariance estimates. With limited transaction frequency, estimating property-to-property covariances directly is often infeasible.

iv. **Discrete (non-divisible) asset allocation:** Classical mean-variance optimization assumes continuous weights, that is, an investor can hold 3.7% of each asset. Real estate's indivisibility means an investor either owns a property (100%) or does not (0%) (Byrne & Lee, 2004).

## 2.4 Heuristics in Investment Decision-Making

The preceding sections have established that property portfolio selection is a decision problem of genuine difficulty. Different factors such as indivisible assets, opaque pricing, limited transaction histories, and high cost of acquiring information combine to create an environment where formal optimization inputs are rarely available in the required quantity or quality. The natural question that follows is: how do investment managers actually decide? The answer is that they rely on simplified decision rules that reduce the complexity of the selection problem to a manageable set of criteria. These decision rules are called heuristics. This section examines the theoretical foundations of heuristic decision-making, distinguishing between two major traditions that make opposing predictions about their consequences, before examining each of the four heuristics central to this study in detail.

### 2.4.1 Bounded Rationality and Satisficing

Herbert Simon's (1955) foundational challenge to the assumption of perfect rationality represents a good starting point for understanding why investment managers use heuristics. Simon argues that the classical concept of *Homo Economicus*, an ever-rational economic agent with complete information that always makes choices that maximises expected utility, is implausible. According to Simon, real decision-makers operate under three binding constraints: cognitive limitations, which cap the volume of information the human mind can process simultaneously; information constraints, which means that complete and reliable data about all decision alternatives is rarely available; and time constraints, which impose practical limits on how long deliberation can continue before a decision must be made.

Of the three constraints Simon identifies, information scarcity is arguably the most acute in the Nigerian context. This is not because time is unlimited or cognition is unconstrained, but because even unlimited time and perfect cognitive capacity simply cannot compensate for data that does not exist.

Simon argued further that in light of the above constraints, decision makers do not optimize; they satisfice (Simon, 1956). Satisficing involves searching through alternatives sequentially until one is found that meets a minimum acceptable standard (Simon, 1955). The standard itself adapts over time, rising when acceptable alternatives are easily found, and falling when search proves prolonged and diﬀicult (March & Simon, 1958).

For property portfolio selection, satisficing manifests as the application of simplified selection rules that quickly identify a pool of acceptable properties from a large universe of candidates. These rules are the heuristics that this study seeks to identify, measure, and evaluate.

### 2.4.2 The Heuristics and Biases Programme (Kahneman & Tversky, 1974)

While Simon's bounded rationality framework explains why heuristics may be used, Tversky and Kahneman's research programme (1974) established what forms they take and what errors they produce. This research demonstrated that individuals rely on a small number of general-purpose heuristics when making probabilistic judgements; specifically, representativeness, availability, and anchoring and adjustment. While these heuristics are often useful and efficient, they produce systematic, predictable errors when applied to problems for which they are poorly suited.

The empirical reach of this programme extends directly into investment decision-making. Barberis and Thaler (2003) provided a comprehensive survey documenting how Kahneman-Tversky heuristics manifest in observed equity market anomalies. Shiller (2003) argued that irrational exuberance in both stock and real estate markets reflects the systematic operation of availability and representativeness heuristics at scale. What is significant about this body of evidence for the present study is its implication that cognitive biases are not eliminated by professional training or institutional sophistication, as even expert decision-makers are shown to exhibit heuristic tendencies.

### 2.4.3 Ecological Rationality: The Case for Heuristics (Gigerenzer et al., 1999)

The Kahneman-Tversky tradition treats heuristics as cognitive errors to be corrected against the standard of normative rationality. A competing tradition, associated with Gigerenzer, Todd and the ABC Research Group (1999), argues that this framing is fundamentally misleading. The ecological rationality principle holds that a heuristic is not best evaluated against an abstract normative ideal, but against the specific environmental structure in which decisions are made. A simple rule that ignores much of the available information may be highly effective in a noisy, data-scarce environment precisely because it is robust to estimation error. Conversely, a sophisticated optimization model that attempts to use all available information may perform poorly when its inputs are unreliable (Gigerenzer & Gaissmaier, 2011). This argument is not merely theoretical. DeMiguel et al. (2009) tested fourteen portfolio optimization models against a naïve equal-weighting strategy across multiple datasets and found that no optimization model consistently outperformed the simpler alternative out-of-sample. This was because estimation error in the optimization inputs more than offset the theoretical gains. Tu and Zhou (2011) formalized this, showing that naïve diversification dominates mean-variance optimization whenever the number of historical observations is less than (N+2)(N+5), where N is the number of assets.

The ecological rationality perspective does not, however, provide unconditional support for heuristic use. Kahneman and Klein (2009) argued that genuine intuitive expertise requires a decision environment with regular, learnable patterns and sufficiently rapid feedback to allow calibration over time. Nigerian institutional real estate satisfies neither condition well. Transactions are infrequent, prices are opaque, and the feedback cycle from acquisition to observable return may span years. The consequence is that experience in this market may not systematically improve heuristic quality. The two traditions thus converge on an empirical question rather than a theoretical conclusion: in the specific data environment of Nigerian institutional property investment, do the heuristics employed by PFAs approximate optimal selection closely enough to be considered adaptive, or do they impose a measurable and correctable performance cost? This is the question that the empirical chapters of this study are designed to answer.

### 2.4.4 The Four Heuristics of Focus

The four heuristics examined in this study (anchoring, availability, representativeness, and herding) were selected on the basis of their prominence in the behavioural finance and real estate literature and their plausibility within the specific institutional context of Nigerian PFA property selection. Each one is examined below in turn, backed up by evidence from the literature, its and anticipated manifestation in the Nigerian PFA context.

### 2.4.4.1 Anchoring and Adjustment

In the heuristics and biases programme in 1974, Tversky and Kahneman explored anchoring.
They described it as a cognitive tendency to begin from an initial value (the anchor) and to adjust from it when forming a final estimate, with the critical characteristic that adjustment is systematically insufficient. As a result, the anchor exerts disproportionate influence on the final judgment, pulling it toward the starting point even when subsequent evidence would rationally justify a larger revision. Epley and Gilovich (2006) demonstrated that this insufficiency occurs because people stop adjusting as soon as they reach a value they consider plausible, rather than continuing until they reach the normatively correct estimate. The effect is robust: it occurs with expert participants, with incentivized decisions, and even when the anchor is explicitly identified as arbitrary or random.

In the real estate context, Northcraft and Neale (1987) provided direct and highly cited evidence of anchoring in property valuation. Professional estate agents who were shown the same property with different listing prices produced valuations similar to those listing prices by a substantial margin, despite reporting that the listing price had not influenced their judgement. The implications for PFA decision-making are significant as valuations commissioned from external surveyors are themselves likely to be anchored to asking prices. This means that the PFA's investment committee may receive an anchored input at the very start of its deliberation. In the Nigerian context, where independent comparable sales data is sparse and external surveyors may rely heavily on the client's stated price expectations, this anchoring chain is likely to be longer and more influential than in markets with transparent transaction databases (Iroham et al., 2013a; Olapade et al., 2019).

### 2.4.4.2 Availability Heuristic

A PFA Investment officer based in Lagos is physically embedded within the Lagos Island real
estate market. He/she travels past prime office developments in Victoria Island and frequently comes across listings in Ikoyi on social media. Lagos Island will be over-represented in his/her property portfolio, not necessarily because Lagos Island is necessarily superior on risk-adjusted terms, but because it is more mentally accessible. This is a textbook scenario of the availability heuristic.

Explored by Tversky and Kahneman (1973), the availabilty heuristic is defined as the tendency to judge the probability or frequency of events by the ease with which instances come to mind. Easily recalled events are judged as more probable or more prevalent than events that are difficult to retrieve from memory. In investment contexts, availability manifests as a systematic preference for familiar, frequently-discussed, or recently prominent assets. French and Poterba (1991) documented this pattern at the level of national equity markets. They noted that investors in every country surveyed held disproportionately large shares of domestic equities, despite the demonstrable diversification benefits available from international exposure. They concluded that since domestic markets are more visible, more frequently discussed in local media, and more easily recalled than foreign alternatives, investors are more likely to invest in them. This is consistent with Tversky & Kahneman’s (1973) definition.

In the illustration above, while Lagos Island may exhibit favourable characteristics for property portfolios, it just as well might not. The heuristic and rational choice may not always point to the same direction. The absence of rationality and evidence in the decision is the point to note, and this is precisely what makes this phenomenon worth studying.

### 2.4.4.3 Representativeness Heuristic

In 1972, Kahneman and Tversky introduced the representativeness heuristic. It describes the
tendency of people to determine that an object belongs to a category, or that an event was
produced by a process, based on the degree to which it resembles the prototype of that category or process. Representativeness leads to a cluster of related errors: insensitivity to base rates, insensitivity to sample size, and the failure to account for regression to the mean. The consequences of representativeness have been demonstrated in investment contexts, specifically by De Bondt and Thaler (1985). According to them, equity investors over-extrapolate recent performance, treating a period of strong returns as representative of a genuinely superior asset and therefore overpaying, before the inevitable mean reversion imposes losses.

In property portfolio selection, representativeness is expected to manifest as sector or location momentum chasing. Given the low transaction frequency in Nigerian institutional real estate, a submarket that has delivered above-average capital appreciation in the most recently observable period may be treated as representative of a superior investment destination, with little scrutiny of whether the recent performance is sustainable or not. This tendency is particularly consequential here in Nigeria because due to the lack of data, the evidence base for any inference is thin. A decision-maker who observes two years of strong appreciation in a given submarket has very little basis for distinguishing genuine structural strength from a temporary market cycle. This means that portfolio selection will track recent performances rather than risk-adjusted return estimates.

### 2.4.4.4 Herding Behaviour

An investment manager who makes an underperforming unconventional decision would expect to face more severe reputational consequences than one whose conventional decision
underperforms by the same amount. This was the conclusion reached by Scharfstein and Stein (1990) when they studied institutional-investment decision-makers. This asymmetry arguably creates a rational incentive to mimic peer institutions, independent of any informational argument. This is what is known as **Herding**.

Studies on herding behaviour came later than the other types of heuristics. Bikhchandani, Hirshleifer and Welch (1992) modelled it as a phenomenon where each decision-maker observes the actions of predecessors and, recognizing that others may possess information superior to their own, rationally updates toward their choices. When this dynamic operates across similar populations, the result is that private information is suppressed and collective decisions converge on a narrow set of options. This convergence is not because those options are objectively superior, but because each individual's observation of others' choices reinforces the same conclusion. Scharfstein and Stein (1990) identified a complementary mechanism specific to institutional investment, namely career-motivated herding. An investment manager whose unconventional decision underperforms would face more severe reputational consequences than one whose conventional decision underperforms by the same amount.

In the Nigerian PFA sector, The five largest PFAs by assets under management control approximately 60% of the sector (PenCom, 2024), and smaller PFAs may reasonably infer that these larger institutions have conducted more extensive due diligence on the properties they hold. Observing that a major PFA has acquired assets in a particular submarket is therefore an informative signal, not merely an arbitrary reference point. Similarly, PFA investment officers who diverge from peer practice in ways that produce underperformance could face both regulatory scrutiny and personal accountability issues while those who follow the crowd face reduced individual exposure even when collective performance is poor. Together, these mechanisms predict that Nigerian PFA property portfolios will cluster in the same locations, the same asset types, and even the same specific properties.

## 2.5 Theoretical Framework

The literature reviewed in Sections 2.2 through 2.4 draws from three distinct theoretical groups, each of which occupies a specific position in the analytical architecture of this study. These groups stand in tension with one another, and the resolution of that tension through empirical investigation is the primary contribution of the study.

The first group is headlined by Modern Portfolio Theory, originating with Markowitz (1952) and extended by Sharpe (1964; 1966; 1994). This group defines the normative benchmark: what an optimally constructed real estate portfolio would look like if all inputs were available and all constraints were correctly specified. Mean-variance optimization provides the efficient frontier against which actual heuristic-driven portfolios will be compared, and the Sharpe ratio provides the primary metric of risk-adjusted performance.

The second group comprises the theoretical explanations for why selection deviates from the normative benchmark. Simon's (1955; 1956) bounded rationality theory establishes the conditions under which simplification becomes a rational response. Akerlof's (1970) information asymmetry model contextualizes why these constraints are particularly acute in property markets. The systematic advantage of sellers over buyers in information access creates structural pressure toward rules-of-thumb that partially substitute for unattainable private information. Tversky and Kahneman's (1974) heuristics-and-biases programme identifies the specific cognitive mechanisms through which that simplification occurs.

The third group contains competing theories about the consequences of deviation. The Kahneman-Tversky perspective predicts that heuristics impose measurable performance costs, as portfolios will lie inside the efficient frontier, and the distance from the frontier will be attributable to the systematic biases each heuristic introduces. The ecological rationality perspective, advanced by Gigerenzer et al. (1999) and empirically supported by DeMiguel et al. (2009) in the portfolio context, predicts that in data-scarce environments, the performance gap may be smaller than theory suggests, or may even close entirely in favour of the simpler approach. The competing empirical predictions provided by these theories creates a tension that can only be arbitrated by evidence from the specific market and institutional context in question.

## 2.6 Empirical Review

The empirical literature directly relevant to this study is organized below by research objective. Within each sub-section, studies are presented broadly in chronological order, and each sub-section closes with a statement of the specific gap that the corresponding objective addresses.

### 2.6.1 Studies on Heuristics in Institutional Investment

The systematic study of heuristic patterns in institutional investment has a short but consequential history. Theoretical explanation for herding was provided by Scharfstein and Stein (1990), whose career-risk model of herding demonstrated that rational agents facing reputational consequences will mimic peers even when private information recommends a different course. Lakonishok, Shleifer and Vishny (1992) documented herding and window-dressing in US pension fund equity portfolios, finding that institutional funds traded in patterns not explained by common public information. This established that even sophisticated, regulated institutions exhibit tendencies to deviate from rationality. Clark and Urwin (2008) extended this inquiry to the governance dimension, finding that the quality of pension fund decision-making processes was the primary determinant of whether investment decisions approached optimality. Funds with weak governance were more susceptible to herding and other heuristic tendencies, while funds with strong governance were more analytically grounded.

Within the African context, Lowies, et al. (2016) documented strong statistical evidence of anchoring and adjustment heuristics among South-African property fund managers.

### 2.6.2 Studies on Comparative Performance: Heuristics vs Optimized Portfolios

Different studies have explicitly compared portfolio performance under rule-based versus optimization-based selection in real estate or closely related contexts. These studies have yielded different results which are discussed below.

Mueller and Ziering (1992) found that US institutional property portfolios constructed using simple sector allocation rules exhibited approximately 22% higher annual volatility than mean-variance optimized portfolios targeting equivalent expected returns. This demonstrates that heuristic selection imposed a measurable risk penalty without any compensating return advantage. Steinert and Crowe (2001) compared optimized and rule-based international property portfolio strategies across fifteen countries and found that mean-variance optimization produced a Sharpe ratio of 0.64 against 0.51 for the rule-based approach.

Further evidence is provided by Lee and Stevenson (2006), who found that prime-location rule-based strategies outperformed optimization-based diversification for Irish institutional property portfolios during 1994–2004. Research by Olaleye et al. (2008) and De Miguel et al. (2009) reinforce this across different market contexts, finding rule-based strategies to be robust enough to compete and even outperform optimal portfolios out of sample.

### 2.6.3 Research Gaps

Drawing together the evidence from Sections 2.6.1 and 2.6.2 above, three research gaps can be identified, each corresponding directly to one of this study's objectives:

**Gap I (Objective I: Identify heuristics):** No published study has empirically documented, through primary research with investment practitioners, the specific heuristics employed by Nigerian Pension Fund Administrators in property portfolio selection. International evidence identifies the likely candidates but cannot confirm which are most prevalent in the Nigerian institutional context, nor in what form they manifest given the specific regulatory, informational, and governance environment of the Nigerian pension industry.

**Gap II (Objective II: Comparative performance):** The comparative performance of heuristic-driven and optimal property portfolios has been examined in developed markets with established transaction histories and performance indices. No comparable study exists for Nigeria specifically for heuristics. The question of whether the optimization advantage documented in international studies holds in a data-scarce environment is what this study directly addresses.

**Gap III (Objective III: Factors driving heuristics):** While the general determinants of heuristic use in institutional investment have been examined, the specific factors that explain heuristic reliance among Nigerian PFA investment managers have not been investigated through primary research. The answer to this gap has implications for what interventions would most effectively improve decision quality.

## 2.7 Conceptual Framework

This chapter's literature review establishes a clear analytical argument that can be distilled into three statements: a) the Nigerian property market presents genuine informational and structural challenges that create conditions under which simplified decision rules are both understandable and potentially costly; b) the four heuristics examined in this study are the mechanisms through which those simplified rules operate; and c) two theoretical traditions make competing predictions about whether those rules impose a correctable performance cost or represent an adaptive response to environmental constraints. The empirical work of Chapters Three and Four is designed to resolve this competition through direct measurement.

The study's conceptual framework, illustrated in Figure 2.1 below, captures these relationships in a single pathway diagram. The framework begins with the two categories of conditions that create the decision environment within which PFA property selection occurs: a) environmental conditions: the features of the Nigerian property market and b) institutional conditions: the governance structures, regulatory constraints, and career incentives of the PFA investment context. Together, these conditions create a decision environment characterized by high information costs, slow feedback, and structural pressure toward simplified selection strategies.

Within this environment, the four heuristics of focus operate as selection mechanisms, translating decision pressure into specific portfolio choices. Anchoring constrains the range of consideration and ties investment decisions to prior reference points. The availability heuristic concentrates selection in geographically and informationally familiar markets. Representativeness drives momentum-based sector allocation. Herding produces a portfolio similar to peer fund holdings. Each heuristic leaves a measurable signature in the resulting portfolio composition, and the study's questionnaire (Objective I) is designed to identify the relative prevalence and weighting of each.

The heuristic portfolio is constructed from a synthetic property universe through the weighted calibration of the questionnaire findings and then compared against the mean-variance optimized portfolio constructed from the same property universe. This comparison (Objective II and III) produces the Sharpe ratio differential, the diversification ratio gap, and the return and risk metrics that allow the study to determine whether heuristics impose a correctable cost or prove adaptive in this specific market context. The findings inform the theoretical resolution at the base of the framework: if optimization significantly outperforms, the Kahneman-Tversky prediction is supported; if heuristics match optimization within the practical significance threshold, the ecological rationality prediction is supported. Each outcome represents an empirical contribution to the debate documented in Section 2.5.

Rather than pre-determining an expected outcome, this study approaches the comparative performance of the heuristic and optimized portfolios as an open question. While the balance of evidence from international comparative studies documents that optimization generally outperforms heuristic selection (Mueller & Ziering, 1992; Steinert & Crowe, 2001; Bond & Mitchell, 2010), the severe data opacity and volatility of the Nigerian market create conditions where those mathematical advantages may erode. As DeMiguel et al. (2009) demonstrate, high estimation errors can neutralize optimization benefits, granting theoretical credibility to Gigerenzer's ecological rationality framework. Therefore, this conceptual framework maintains strict neutrality. The empirical results detailed in Chapter Four will independently dictate whether the Nigerian institutional context aligns with the predictions of the heuristics-and-biases programme or the ecological rationality paradigm.
