# INTRODUCTION

## 1.1 Background to the Study

Pension Fund Administrators (PFAs) play a critical role in modern financial systems as they manage the retirement savings of millions of investors. The investment mandate held by these institutions extends beyond the mere conservation of capital to include the investment of this pooled capital across various asset classes (equities, fixed income securities, infrastructure, and notably, real estate). The aim of this investment is to generate sustained levels of risk-adjusted returns over extended time periods (Blake, 2003; Andonov et al., 2017). Among the various asset classes mentioned above, property or real estate investments are particularly attractive due to their distinct characteristics: potential for long-term capital appreciation, capacity for income generation through rental yields, inflation-hedging potential, and low correlation with traditional financial assets (Geltner et al., 2014).

The Nigerian Pension Industry has experienced substantial growth in asset valuation, a surge from ₦1.58 trillion in 2009 to approx. ₦26 trillion by the third quarter of 2025 (PenCom, 2025). This exponential growth has required the diversification of assets beyond traditional concentration in Federal Government securities. To this end, the recently-revised regulation on investment of pension fund assets explicitly authorizes PFAs to invest up to 10% of their portfolios to real estate and infrastructure, and up to 15% to Real Estate Investment Trusts (REITs), reflective of real estate’s strategic importance in long-term portfolio construction (PenCom, 2025).

Despite the regulatory allowances for broader real estate exposure, observational evidence reveals a continual implementation gap between the upper limits of regulatory permissions and actual allocation percentages. Nigerian PFA’s actual allocations to real estate consistently remain below the permitted thresholds, typically ranging between 0.9% and 3.5% of their asset portfolios (PenCom Quarterly Reports, 2020 - 2025). This phenomenon is often attributed to the inherent characteristics of real estate as an asset class, colloquially termed “lumpiness”, referring to the perpetual indivisibility, high costs, prolonged transaction periods, and illiquidity that distinguish real estate from other asset classes (Baum & Hartzell, 2012; Ling, 2005).

In addition to the underlying negative characteristics of real estate as an asset class, the Nigerian real estate market also possesses further intricacies, adding an extra layer of complexity to these inherent characteristics. Unlike developed markets with mature Real Estate Investment Trusts (REITs), established property indices and transparent transaction databases, the Nigerian property market is fundamentally opaque (JLL, 2024). It is characterized by significant information asymmetry, title documentation challenges, and the marked absence of trusted property data (Olapade et al., 2019; Onwuanyi, 2020).

As a result of these conditions, investment decision-makers frequently employ heuristics (mental shortcuts, rules of thumb, or simplified decision rules) that serve to reduce cognitive load and facilitate quick judgments and decision-making within complex, information-scarce environments (Gigerenzer & Gaissmaier, 2011). These heuristics may manifest in various forms, namely: anchoring (Northcraft & Neale, 1987); availability (Tversky & Khaneman, 1973); representativeness (Khaneman & Tversky, 1972) and herding (Bikhchandani et al., 1992; Salzman & Zwinkels, 2013), among others.

While existing research has examined the usage of major heuristics in Nigerian property investment valuation and its affecting factors (Iroham et al., 2013), property portfolio diversification strategies (Olaleye, 2008, 2011), and real estate performance in Nigerian pension fund portfolios (Okeahialam & Awa, 2024; Okeahialam & Ezema, 2024), a significant gap remains. Specifically, no study has yet proven whether heuristics prove to be effective for property portfolio selection decisions in opaque, data-scarce environments.

This study addresses this critical gap and answers the salient question of whether the use of heuristics in property portfolio selection is causing PFAs to leave money on the table, or if the use thereof represents a proper adaptation to the peculiarities of the Nigerian property market.

## 1.2 Statement of the Research Problem

The central aim of constructing a real estate portfolio for pension funds is to identify a ‘selection’ that maximizes risk-adjusted returns over the investment period, while constrained by certain factors like liquidity, regulatory requirements and the fund’s specific liability structure (Amenc et al., 2009; Hoevenaars et al., 2008). This is especially vital for PFAs due to the long-term obligations they owe to contributors.

Modern Portfolio Theory provides a practical framework for this optimization: Given a universe of available properties with estimated returns and a covariance matrix capturing risk relationships, the efficient frontier identifies portfolio selections offering maximum expected returns for specified risk levels, or equivalently, minimum risk for target returns thresholds (Markowitz, 1952; Merton, 1972).

In data scarce, emerging markets like Nigeria, however, the efficacy of Modern Portfolio Theory becomes implausible. The inherent conditions within these markets create an environment where the theoretical assumptions that Modern Portfolio Theory is built on are systematically violated (Shiller, 2003; Kahneman, 2011). Resultantly, portfolio decisions by institutional investors, such as PFAs, often deviate from the recommendations of Modern Portfolio Theory. Research from Iroham (2013) extensively documented a number of these derivative patterns within the context of the Nigerian real estate market: i. Properties may be selected based on anchoring to valuations provided by surveyors, even when these valuations inadequately reflect market fundamentals or incorporate backward-looking data (Northcraft & Neale, 1987). ii. Portfolio construction exhibits availability bias, through the selection of properties in highly visible, frequently-transacted locations (Lagos Island: Ikoyi, Victoria Island; Abuja: Maitama, Asokoro) despite potentially superior risk-adjusted opportunities in less-prominent submarkets. iii. Investment decisions demonstrate herding behaviour, with PFAs mimicking the property selections of competitors or following “best practices” that may not align with the specific fund’s risk-return preferences or liability structure.

The central research problem is that while the prevalence of these heuristics is well-established narratively and supported by international evidence, their consequences within the Nigerian pension fund context remain empirically undetermined. To address the research problem, this study poses the following questions:

i. What specific heuristics are commonly employed by Nigerian Pension Fund Administrators in property portfolio selection decisions? ii. How do heuristic-driven portfolios perform relative to mean-variance optimized portfolios over similar holding periods? iii. What factors influence the use of heuristics among Nigerian PFA investment managers?

## 1.3 Aim and Objectives of the Study

The aim of this study is to examine the role of heuristics in property portfolio selection decisions of Pension Fund Administrators in Nigeria with a view to assessing whether heuristics prove adaptive for property portfolio selection.

To achieve this aim, the study pursues the following specific objectives to:

i. identify the types of heuristics most frequently employed by Nigerian PFA investment managers in the property selection and portfolio construction process

ii. construct representative property portfolios from both heuristic-driven decision rules derived from survey findings and mean-variance optimization techniques adapted for discrete, indivisible real estate assets

iii. conduct a comparative performance evaluation of heuristic-driven and mean-variance optimized portfolios

iv. examine the factors that influence the use of heuristics among Nigerian PFA investors

## 1.4 Justification of the Study

This study is justified on theoretical, contextual and practical grounds, contributing to both theoretical knowledge and professional practice.

Theoretically, this study makes distinct contributions to real estate portfolio theory. As observed in various research publications by distinguished scholars (Olaleye, 2011; Olaleye & Aluko, 2007), real estate portfolio theory has its theoretical roots in Modern Portfolio Theory. This study presents a different perspective, examining other factors and biases that feature during the property portfolio construction process. It also provides a methodological template for measuring the role and efficacy of heuristics within emerging, opaque markets.

To paint the contextual picture further, Nigeria’s pension industry is one of Africa’s largest pools of institutional capital with approx. ₦26 trillion in assets under management (RisCura, 2020; PenCom, 2025). For an industry of this scale, less than 1% of total asset value in real estate investments, as observed in the PenCom 2025 Q3 reports, signals underdevelopment. As such, this research addresses a gap that is not merely theoretical but has significant implications for retirement income security of millions of contributors.

From a practical perspective, the study’s findings have direct implications for multiple stakeholder groups. For Pension Fund Administrators, the research provides measurable evidence on the efficacy of current property portfolio selection decision-making practices. Depending on the result of the findings, PFA investment managers can streamline their property portfolio selection decision framework towards achieving better returns. For regulators (PenCom), findings inform whether the underutilization of current limits are reflective of the property market’s realities or is simply due to behavioural patterns. Depending on the result, the

regulators can assess whether PFAs possess adequate analytical capacity, or if industry-wide benchmarks are needed.

## 1.5 Scope of the Study

The study focuses on direct real estate investments within the portfolios managed by Pension Fund Administrators, excluding REITs, development projects, and other indirect property investments. REITs and other indirect property investments are excluded because they involve different decision processes than direct property investment. REITs introduce market pricing mechanisms that reduce information asymmetry and enable liquidity-driven trading (Geltner et al., 2014). Development projects are also known to introduce construction risk and timeline uncertainty (Baum, 2015). As seen from the evidence above, focusing on direct investment serves to isolate the specific decision context that this study seeks to examine.

The study is limited to PFAs licensed by PenCom only, and excludes PFCs and informal fund managers. This decision takes root in the statutory fact that PFAs are the investment decision-making entities in the Pension Industry; PFCs are simply custodians without investment directives (PRA, 2014; Faponhunda, 2013). Since the research examines investment decision-making heuristics, it follows that the scope is delimited to the domain of PFAs. Unlicensed or informal fund managers operate outside the scope of the Pencom regulatory framework that forms the study context.

Finally, the research utilizes a fully-synthetic property data approach. While this is majorly due to the data opacity and absence of standardized historical property-level performance indices,

this choice allows for a controlled environment for specific isolation and rigorous testing of heuristics, while also being consistent with established computational finance practices for data-scarce environments (Glasserman, 2004; Campbell et al., 1997).

## 1.6 Definition of Terms

**Heuristics:** Cognitive strategies or simplified decision rules that reduce complex problems to manageable judgments through shortcuts rather than exhaustive analysis (Tversky & Kahneman, 1974; Gigerenzer & Gaissmaier, 2011).

**Anchoring:** A cognitive bias whereby individuals rely disproportionately on the first piece of information encountered (the “anchor”) when making subsequent judgments (Tversky & Kahneman, 1974). In real estate, anchoring commonly occurs when valuations or asking prices unduly influence assessments (Northcraft & Neale, 1987; Babawale, 2013).

**Availability Heuristic:** A mental shortcut relying on immediate examples that come to mind when evaluating decisions (Tversky & Kahneman, 1973). Individuals overweight vivid, recent, or easily recalled information, manifesting as preference for properties in highly visible locations.

**Herding Behaviour:** The tendency to mimic peer investment decisions rather than acting on independent analysis (Bikhchandani, Hirshleifer & Welch, 1992).

**Mean-Variance Optimization (MVO):** A quantitative portfolio construction technique, originating with Markowitz (1952), that selects allocations to maximize expected return for given risk (variance) or minimize risk for target return, identifying the “efficient frontier.”

**Efficient Frontier:** The set of optimal portfolios in mean-variance space, representing maximum attainable expected return for each risk level (Markowitz, 1952).

**Sharpe Ratio:** A risk-adjusted return measure, calculated as (R - Rf) / σ, where R is portfolio mean return, Rf is risk-free rate, and σ is standard deviation (Sharpe, 1966, 1994).

**Pension Fund Administrator (PFA):** Licensed entities authorized by PenCom to manage pension assets under the Contributory Pension Scheme (Pension Reform Act 2004, amended 2014), with fiduciary obligations to invest prudently.

**Property Universe:** A comprehensive set of synthetic properties from which all portfolios used within this research are constructed.

**Backtest Period:** The specific historical period from which real-world market data was gathered to construct property portfolios and test their performance.
