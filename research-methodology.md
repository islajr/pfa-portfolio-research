## SECTION 3.1 — Introduction (Preamble)

### Function
Reorients the reader from Chapter Two's theoretical discussion to the empirical plan. Establishes the methodological logic before any specific choices are described. Defends the overall approach and pre-empts the three questions most likely to be raised: "Why mixed methods?", "Why synthetic data?", and "Is this really rigorous?"

### What to Write

**Paragraph 1 — Restatement of aim and the methodological challenge it creates.**
Begin by restating the study's aim in one sentence: *"This study aims to examine the role of heuristics in property portfolio selection decisions of Nigerian Pension Fund Administrators, with a view to identifying the types and prevalence of heuristic decision-making, evaluating their performance implications relative to quantitatively optimized alternatives, and understanding the factors that drive their continued use in institutional real estate investment."* Then establish immediately that this aim creates a methodological challenge: the four research questions are not of the same type. RQ1 and RQ4 ask *what* and *why* — they require behavioural identification through primary research with human participants. RQ2 and RQ3 ask *how much* — they require quantitative performance measurement through computational analysis. A single-method design cannot address both. This establishes the necessity of a mixed-methods approach before you name it.

**Paragraph 2 — The mixed-methods rationale.**
State that this study adopts a mixed-methods research design, combining primary survey data collection with computational portfolio simulation. Explain that the combination is not used for completeness or comprehensiveness — it is imposed by the structure of the research questions themselves. Cite Creswell & Plano Clark (2018) ⭐🟡 — *Designing and Conducting Mixed Methods Research* (SAGE, 3rd ed.) — as the standard methodological reference for mixed-methods design. Alternatively, cite Tashakkori & Teddlie (2010) ⭐🟡 — *SAGE Handbook of Mixed Methods in Social and Behavioral Research* — if you can verify access. If neither is accessible, the justification can stand on the internal logic of the research questions without a citation, but having one strengthens the section.

**Paragraph 3 — The synthetic data strategy: pre-emptive defence.**
Before describing any specific choice, address the data environment directly. State that the Nigerian institutional real estate market presents a structural data challenge: there is no publicly accessible transaction database for the type of properties PFAs invest in (Grade A offices, commercial plazas, industrial estates with values above ₦150M), no published performance index comparable to NCREIF or MSCI/IPD, and no historical return series at the individual property level. This is not a limitation of this study — it is a documented characteristic of the market (PenCom Annual Reports, 2022–2024 🟢; Knight Frank Nigeria, 2024 🟢). The methodological response is a calibrated simulation approach, in which a synthetic property universe is generated under statistical parameters derived from documented real market publications. This approach is established in computational finance literature (Glasserman, 2004 ⭐🟡 — *Monte Carlo Methods in Financial Engineering*, Springer; Campbell, Lo & MacKinlay, 1997 ⭐🟡 — *The Econometrics of Financial Markets*, Princeton University Press) and has been applied specifically to real estate portfolio research in data-scarce environments (DeMiguel, Garlappi & Uppal, 2009 🟢; Steinert & Crowe, 2001 🟡). State this confidently and without apology. Do not use hedging language like "given the limitations of available data, this study was forced to…" — the correct framing is "the calibrated simulation approach was selected because it is methodologically appropriate for this asset class in this market."

**Paragraph 4 — Chapter roadmap.**
List Sections 3.2 through 3.5 with a one-sentence description of each. Keep this brief — it is navigational, not substantive.

### ⚠️ Watch-Outs
- Do not reproduce material from Section 1.2 (Research Problem) or Section 1.3 (Objectives) at length. A one-sentence restatement of the aim is sufficient — you are orienting the reader, not re-arguing the case for the study.
- Do not describe the questionnaire or the universe generator here. This section is about the *why* of the overall design; sections 3.4 and 3.3 respectively are about the *what* and *how*.
- Do not be defensive about synthetic data. The moment you frame it as a compromise, you invite the examiner to treat it as one.

## SECTION 3.2 — Research Design

### Function
Establishes the philosophical and strategic positioning of the research. Answers three questions in sequence: what worldview does this study operate from (paradigm)?; what kind of design does it use (design type)?; and what strategy does it employ (research strategy)? Each answer must be accompanied by a justification that connects it to the study's specific requirements — not generic statements like "mixed methods is flexible."

### What to Write

#### 3.2.1 — Research Paradigm

**The pragmatic paradigm.**
State that this study operates within a **pragmatic** philosophical paradigm. Pragmatism holds that research questions, not philosophical commitments, determine the choice of methods (Creswell & Plano Clark, 2018 ⭐🟡; Tashakkori & Teddlie, 2010 ⭐🟡). A pragmatist researcher selects quantitative, qualitative, or mixed methods based on what is needed to answer the research questions, without committing to the epistemological assumptions of either pure positivism (which would demand all data be numerically observable) or pure interpretivism (which would demand all meaning be socially constructed). Justify the choice: this study requires both behavioural identification (which has interpretivist dimensions — understanding how investment managers reason) and quantitative performance measurement (which has positivist dimensions — computing Sharpe ratios and testing statistical hypotheses). Neither pure positivism nor pure interpretivism can accommodate both requirements. Pragmatism can.

#### 3.2.2 — Research Design

**Mixed-methods comparative design.**
State that the study adopts a **mixed-methods comparative design**. The mixed-methods dimension reflects the combination of survey-based qualitative and quantitative primary research (for Objectives I and IV) with computational simulation and quantitative analysis (for Objectives II and III). The comparative dimension reflects the study's central analytical structure: constructing two portfolios from the same property universe and comparing their performance. A comparative design requires that the things being compared are drawn from the same population, assessed against the same criteria, and evaluated using pre-specified metrics — all of which this study achieves by using a common property universe and a standardized performance metrics suite.

**The sequential design structure.**
Within the mixed-methods framework, the design is **sequential**: the survey component (Phase 1) is conducted first, and its findings — specifically the heuristic weights derived from questionnaire responses — inform the parameters of the computational component (Phase 2). The composite heuristic scores from Phase 1 calibrate the scoring weights (α₁, α₂, α₃, α₄) in the heuristic portfolio construction algorithm used in Phase 2. This connection means the two phases are not merely parallel strands that happen to share a report — the survey genuinely feeds the computation. Make this connection explicit here, and carry it through to Sections 3.4 and 3.5.

#### 3.2.3 — Research Strategy

**Simulation-based comparative case study.**
State that the study employs a **simulation-based comparative case study strategy**, treating the Nigerian PFA sector as the institutional case and the synthetic property universe as a controlled experimental environment within it. The case study element provides contextual depth — the study is not seeking to generalize across all institutional real estate markets but to understand the specific decision-making patterns of a defined institutional population (Nigerian PFAs) within a defined market context (Nigerian institutional real estate). The simulation element provides the controlled comparison — by holding the property universe constant and varying only the selection method (heuristic vs. optimized), the study isolates the effect of the decision approach from the effect of market conditions.

Cite Yin (2018) ⭐🟡 — *Case Study Research and Applications* (SAGE, 6th ed.) — for the case study framework. This is one of the most widely cited methodology texts in social science and is almost certainly real; verify on WorldCat before citing. For the simulation element, cite Glasserman (2004) ⭐🟡 and Campbell, Lo & MacKinlay (1997) ⭐🟡 as above.

### ⚠️ Watch-Outs
- Do not confuse paradigm, design, and strategy — they are different levels of methodological abstraction and your supervisor will notice if you conflate them. Paradigm = philosophical worldview. Design = structural arrangement of the study. Strategy = the specific approach to data generation and analysis.
- Do not say "this study is quantitative" and leave it there. It is mixed-methods, with a predominantly quantitative analytical phase but a qualitatively grounded data collection phase.
- Do not claim that case study research is less rigorous than experimental research. Yin (2018) specifically addresses this mischaracterization. A well-designed case study with clearly specified methods and pre-registered analysis procedures produces credible findings.

This cannot be captured through closed-ended survey items alone; Section D of the questionnaire uses semi-structured open items to elicit this data.

### 3.3.2 — Study Population and Sampling Frame

**The population:**
The study population is all Pension Fund Administrators licensed by the National Pension Commission (PenCom) as of Q1 2026. As at Q3 2024 (the most recent period for which PenCom has published industry statistics), 23 PFAs held valid operating licences (PenCom, 2024 🟢). These 23 entities collectively manage over ₦18 trillion in assets under management and administer approximately 10.2 million Retirement Savings Accounts. List all 23 PFAs by name here, drawn from PenCom's published quarterly reports — this is the complete directory your supervisor specified. A simple table with columns: Fund Name, Headquarters City, Approximate AUM (category: below ₦500B / ₦500B–₦2T / above ₦2T) will satisfy this requirement. The AUM categories can be drawn from PenCom's published data.

**The target sub-population:**
Not all 23 PFAs are equally appropriate targets for this study. The survey specifically concerns property portfolio selection decisions, which presupposes active direct real estate investment activity. Based on PenCom's portfolio classification data in quarterly reports (PenCom Quarterly Reports, 2020–2024 🟢), approximately 15–18 PFAs report direct real estate holdings. PFAs without active real estate programs lack the experiential basis to respond meaningfully to questions about property selection heuristics. The target sub-population is therefore restricted to PFAs with reported direct real estate investment activity in any quarterly period between 2020 and 2024.

**Respondent profile:**
Within each targeted PFA, the study will seek responses from individuals occupying investment decision-making roles. The eligible respondent categories are: Chief Investment Officer (CIO); Head of Portfolio Management; Senior Investment Analyst; Real Estate Investment Analyst; or equivalent title designations. These roles are identified as the loci of property selection authority because PenCom's governance framework (PenCom, 2019 🟢) requires that real estate investment decisions be made or approved by credentialed investment professionals. Administrative, compliance, or operations staff are explicitly excluded because their roles do not include property selection authority.

**Target sample size:**
The target is **20–25 usable individual responses** from **8–10 distinct PFAs**. The rationale for this target:
- A census of all 23 PFAs is not feasible given access constraints inherent in the Nigerian institutional investment context — investment teams are small and inaccessible to unsolicited research requests.
- 8–10 PFAs represents 35–43% of the licensed population. Given that the top five PFAs by AUM control approximately 60% of industry assets (PenCom, 2024 🟢), a sample including two or three of the top five would represent a majority of real estate investment capital even with a modest firm count.
- 20–25 individual responses provides sufficient variance for meaningful cross-tabulation by respondent role, fund size, and experience level (the control variables in the analysis).
- The small-N limitation is explicitly addressed through bootstrap resampling (BCa method, 10,000 replications) in Section 3.5.1, which does not require large samples to produce valid confidence intervals.

**Sampling technique:**
This study employs **purposive sampling** — also termed judgement sampling — supplemented by **snowball sampling** for respondent identification within target funds. Purposive sampling is appropriate when the research question requires specific characteristics in respondents that random sampling cannot efficiently deliver (Patton, 2002 ⭐🟡 — *Qualitative Research and Evaluation Methods*, SAGE; this is a very well-known text, almost certainly real, verify on WorldCat). Here, the required characteristic is direct real estate investment decision authority within a PenCom-licensed PFA — a small, well-defined population that random sampling of the general pension industry workforce would not efficiently reach.

Snowball sampling is applied within funds: an initial contact at each PFA (reached through the study's primary warm-introduction channel — a Pension Fund Custodian contact with active working relationships across multiple PFAs) is asked to recommend other senior investment staff within the same organization who meet the respondent profile. This chain-referral approach is standard in studies of institutional investment professionals (Clark & Urwin, 2008 🟢; Lakonishok, Shleifer & Vishny, 1992 🟢 — both document the use of non-probability sampling in institutional investment research).

**Justification for non-probability sampling:**
Random sampling requires a complete, accessible sampling frame (a list of all eligible units) and a mechanism for reaching any randomly selected unit. Neither condition is met in this population: there is no publicly available directory of PFA investment staff, and cold outreach to senior investment officers of financial institutions yields negligible response rates without institutional facilitation. Non-probability sampling is the methodologically appropriate response to these access constraints and is explicitly acknowledged as a limitation in Section 3.6.

### 3.3.4 — Synthetic Property Universe Design

This sub-section is the longest in Section 3.3 and deserves proportional detail. Its job is to establish that the synthetic universe is not arbitrary — it is a deliberately designed **experimental instrument** whose composition is determined by the research questions, not by convenience.

**The universe as experimental instrument — why design matters:**
For the performance comparison to produce interpretable findings, the universe must be constructed so that the heuristic engine and the optimizer select *different* portfolios. If every property in the universe were a prime Lagos Island property with C of O and excellent condition, both decision processes would converge on similar allocations, and no meaningful comparison would be possible. The universe is therefore deliberately designed to include properties that activate each of the four modelled heuristics — properties that heuristic rules will systematically exclude or under-weight but that formal optimization may select based on risk-return merit. This design principle is not a source of bias; it is the condition that makes the research question testable.

**Universe specification:**

*Size:* The universe will contain **80 institutional-grade synthetic properties**. The rationale for this size: (a) it is large enough to provide the optimizer with genuine choice — given PenCom's 5% concentration cap, a ₦10B fund can hold at most 20 properties at maximum concentration, so the universe of 80 provides a 4:1 candidate-to-selection ratio; (b) it is small enough to remain computationally tractable for binary integer programming, which has NP-hard complexity in the number of binary decision variables; (c) it is consistent with the existing literature on portfolio simulation (DeMiguel, Garlappi & Uppal, 2009 🟢 used similar-scale universes).

*Reproducibility:* The Python random number generator will be seeded at **seed = 42** before any sampling calls. This ensures that the universe is fully reproducible: running the generator script with seed 42 will always produce the identical 80-property dataset. The seed value will be recorded in the database's `generation_config` table and reported in this chapter as a formal parameter of the study.

### 3.4.1 — Survey Instrument Design

**Opening paragraph:**
The primary data collection instrument will be a **structured questionnaire** administered to PFA investment staff meeting the respondent profile established in Section 3.3.2. The questionnaire is structured rather than unstructured because the study requires responses that are directly comparable across respondents and directly translatable into the composite heuristic scores used in Objective I. Fully open-ended interviews would produce richer qualitative data on individual reasoning but would not permit the quantitative scoring and cross-respondent aggregation that the analysis requires. The structured format reflects the sequential mixed-methods design described in Section 3.2.2: the questionnaire must generate both qualitative insight (Section D) and quantifiable heuristic scores (Sections B and C) within a single instrument.

**Section A — Respondent Profile (6 items):**
Purpose: Establishes control variables for cross-tabulation and enables assessment of response quality and representativeness. Items collect: (1) current job title; (2) years of experience in real estate or investment management; (3) fund's approximate AUM (by category: below ₦500B / ₦500B–₦2T / above ₦2T — to protect confidentiality); (4) number of direct real estate properties currently in the fund's portfolio; (5) whether the respondent directly participates in property selection decisions (Yes / Advisory role only / No); and (6) whether the fund uses any formal quantitative model in property selection (Yes, fully / Yes, partially / No — judgment-based). Items 5 and 6 serve as eligibility screeners: responses to subsequent sections are weighted by decision authority level, and the distribution of responses to item 6 directly informs Objective IV.

**Section B — Property Selection Criteria (4 items):**
Purpose: Establishes the *relative importance* respondents assign to different selection criteria, without labelling any criterion as a heuristic. The theory-neutral framing is deliberate — labelling criteria as "anchoring" or "herding" would prime respondents to give socially desirable answers.

Item B1 (Rank-order): Respondents rank eight criteria from 1 (most important) to 8 (least important): (1) location prestige / submarket reputation; (2) title documentation status; (3) rental yield / income return; (4) tenant profile and lease security; (5) physical condition of the property; (6) activity of peer PFAs in the same submarket; (7) valuer's / surveyor's recommendation; (8) market liquidity / ease of future disposal. The rank-order format forces trade-offs: a respondent cannot rate all criteria as equally important. The ranking of criteria 1, 2, and 6 (which map to availability, anchoring, and herding respectively) against criteria 3 and 8 (which map to rational financial analysis) provides direct evidence of heuristic primacy.

Items B2–B4 (Likert, 5-point: 1 = Strongly Disagree; 5 = Strongly Agree): Statements designed to elicit heuristic-consistent responses without naming the heuristic:
- B2: "A property in Ikoyi, Victoria Island, or Maitama carries lower investment risk than an equally-priced property in a less prominent location, regardless of specific financial characteristics." (Availability/anchoring)
- B3: "When a property lacks a Certificate of Occupancy or Governor's Consent, we would not recommend it regardless of how attractive its yield might be." (Anchoring/title filter)
- B4: "Our fund's property selections over the past three years have been broadly similar in location and type to those of other PFAs we are aware of." (Herding)

**Section C — Investment Scenarios (4 items):**
Purpose: Each scenario presents a specific investment decision designed to elicit the presence or absence of one heuristic without naming it. Respondents indicate the more likely course of action on a 5-point scale and provide a brief rationale.

*Scenario C1 — Anchoring:*
"Your fund is evaluating a Grade A office building. An independent valuation conducted four months ago placed the market value at ₦900 million. A current valuation, citing softened market conditions, values the property at ₦730 million. The current asking price is ₦740 million. How strongly does the earlier ₦900M valuation influence the maximum offer your fund would make?"
Scale: 1 = Not at all; 5 = Very strongly (determines ceiling near ₦900M)
Rationale: Tests whether a prior anchor value persists in pricing even when subsequent evidence justifies downward revision — the classic anchoring-and-insufficient-adjustment pattern documented by Northcraft & Neale (1987) 🟢.

*Scenario C2 — Availability (Geographic):*
"Your analysis identifies two commercial properties with statistically equivalent risk-adjusted returns — identical cap rates, comparable title status, similar tenant profiles, and equivalent building specification. Property A is located in the Victoria Island business district. Property B is located in a comparable commercial zone in Ibadan. How likely is your fund to prefer Property A?"
Scale: 1 = No preference; 5 = Strongly prefer A (regardless of equivalent fundamentals)
Rationale: Tests geographic preference beyond financial merit — the availability heuristic predicting preference for the more mentally available (more familiar, more discussed) location.

*Scenario C3 — Representativeness (Momentum):*
"A specific property sector (Grade A office in Abuja) has delivered capital appreciation of 18% in each of the past two years, substantially above the market average of 9%. How much does this recent performance increase your inclination to target this sector for new acquisitions in the current year?"
Scale: 1 = No effect; 5 = Significantly increases inclination (regardless of whether the trend is sustainable)
Rationale: Tests extrapolation from small samples — the representativeness heuristic predicting that a two-year trend will be over-interpreted as evidence of ongoing outperformance, as documented by De Bondt & Thaler (1985) 🟢 in equity markets.

*Scenario C4 — Herding:*
"You become aware that three of the five largest PFAs by AUM have recently acquired Grade A office properties in a specific Abuja submarket. The yield on these acquisitions appears to be slightly below your fund's target return hurdle. How much does the peer activity increase your likelihood of evaluating similar properties in that submarket?"
Scale: 1 = No effect; 5 = Significantly increases likelihood (despite the yield shortfall)
Rationale: Tests information-cascade herding — the tendency to follow peer actions even when private information (the yield shortfall relative to hurdle) would suggest restraint, as theorized by Bikhchandani, Hirshleifer & Welch (1992) 🟢 and Scharfstein & Stein (1990) 🟢.

**Section D — Institutional Context and Constraints (3 items):**
Purpose: Addresses Objective IV. Captures qualitative data on the institutional, informational, and cognitive factors that explain heuristic use. Items are semi-structured to allow free elaboration.

D1 (Multiple-choice with open text): "How are final property selection decisions made at your fund?" Options: Individual investment manager authority / Investment committee approval required / External advisor recommendation required / Board-level approval required / Other (specify). Follow-up: "Please describe the typical process from initial identification to final decision."
D2 (Likert + open text): "To what extent does the absence of reliable, comprehensive transaction data for Nigerian institutional real estate limit the use of formal quantitative analysis in your fund's property selection?" Scale: 1 = Not at all limiting; 5 = Severely limiting. Follow-up: "Please briefly describe the data gaps that most constrain your investment process."
D3 (Open): "What single change in data availability, internal process, or regulatory environment would most improve your fund's property selection decision-making?"

### 3.4.2 — Synthetic Property Universe Generation

**Opening paragraph:**
The property universe will be generated by a purpose-built Python script (`generate_universe.py`) that creates an 80-property synthetic dataset under documented, reproducible statistical parameters. As a data collection instrument, the generator is no less rigorous than a questionnaire: both instruments produce data under pre-specified rules, both have documented parameters, and both can be audited and reproduced. The generator's reproducibility (seed 42) is in fact *more* reproducible than a questionnaire, which relies on the uncontrolled responses of human participants. This framing is important for defending the instrument to your committee — treat the generator as a formal data instrument, not a computational convenience.

**Generation algorithm (high-level, for the dissertation):**
The generator proceeds in the following sequence for each of the 80 properties:

1. *Geographic assignment:* A state, LGA, and micro-location are sampled from the geographic distribution table in Section 3.3.4, using pre-specified probabilities calibrated from PenCom and Knight Frank market activity data.

2. *Asset type assignment:* Asset type is sampled conditional on the state — industrial assets are over-represented in Kano and Rivers; office and commercial assets are over-represented in Lagos Island and Abuja.

3. *Property attributes:* Title status, condition, floor area, year built, occupancy rate, and lease term are sampled from documented distributions specific to each state × asset type cell.

4. *Price generation:* Asking price is sampled from a log-normal distribution, parameterized by a cell-specific median price and standard deviation calibrated from Knight Frank and CBRE price per square metre data. Log-normal is the appropriate distribution for property prices because prices are strictly positive and right-skewed (higher-priced properties are rarer than lower-priced ones). The generator uses resampling (not clamping) to enforce floor and ceiling bounds — this preserves the shape of the distribution rather than creating artificial concentration at the boundaries.

5. *Rent calculation:* Gross rental yield is sampled from a distribution parameterized by asset type and location tier (calibrated from Knight Frank yield tables). Annual rent = price × gross yield.

6. *Market index assignment:* Each property is assigned to exactly one of 15 market indices based on its state and asset type.

7. *Financial metrics calculation:* Total Acquisition Cost (TAC), Net Operating Income (NOI), Cap Rate, Expected Return, Total Volatility, and individual Sharpe Ratio are computed for each property using the formulae specified in Section 3.5.2.

8. *Behavioural flags:* Four boolean fields are computed: `is_prime_location`, `is_preferred_asset_type`, `triggers_anchoring_rule` (price > ₦800M), and `is_heuristic_eligible` (passes all primary heuristic hard filters simultaneously).

**Validation protocol:**
The universe will not be frozen for analysis until it passes all nine validation criteria listed in Table 3.3:

**Table 3.3: Universe Validation Criteria**

| Test | Method | Pass Criterion |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Price log-normality | Kolmogorov-Smirnov test against normal distribution on log(price) | p > 0.05 |
| 2. Price skewness direction | Check skewness coefficient | Skewness > 0 (right-skewed, as expected for prices) |
| 3. Price mean > median | Check moments | Mean exceeds median (right-skew confirmation) |
| 4. Gross yield range | Min/max check | All yields within 4%–14% of price |
| 5. Geographic diversity | State count | ≥ 5 distinct states represented |
| 6. Lagos dominance check | Lagos share | Lagos share < 70% of universe |
| 7. Title diversity | Sub-optimal title share | 20%–40% of universe has non-C of O / non-Gov Consent title |
| 8. Covariance matrix PSD | Minimum eigenvalue check | Minimum eigenvalue ≥ −1×10⁻¹⁰ |
| 9. Heuristic pool size | Count of `is_heuristic_eligible` properties | ≥ 25 properties eligible |

Validation test results will be presented as a formal table in Appendix [X] (Table: Synthetic Property Universe Validation Results). The nine-test framework documents that the universe has the statistical properties required for the analysis and that the heuristic engine will have a sufficient eligible pool from which to construct its portfolio.

### 3.5.1 — Heuristic Identification and Scoring (Objective I)

**Method overview:**
Heuristic prevalence will be measured using composite scores constructed from questionnaire responses. For each of the four heuristics, a composite score is calculated by aggregating the relevant Likert-scale responses (Section B) and scenario responses (Section C), weighted by their theoretical loading on the underlying heuristic construct.

**Composite score formulae:**

*Anchoring Composite Score (ACS):*

$$ACS_k = \frac{w_1 \cdot B3_k + w_2 \cdot C1_k}{w_1 + w_2}$$

Where for respondent *k*: B3ₖ = Likert response to item B3 (title exclusion regardless of yield), normalized to [0,1]; C1ₖ = scenario response to item C1 (anchoring scenario), normalized to [0,1]; w₁ = 0.4; w₂ = 0.6 (scenario responses carry higher weight as they represent revealed rather than stated preferences). Weights are pre-specified based on the literature's preference for revealed over stated measures of cognitive bias (Tversky & Kahneman, 1974 🟢).

*Availability Composite Score (AVCS):*

$$AVCS_k = \frac{w_1 \cdot B1\_rank\_loc_k + w_2 \cdot B2_k + w_3 \cdot C2_k}{w_1 + w_2 + w_3}$$

Where: B1_rank_locₖ = rank assigned to "location prestige" in item B1, inverted and normalized (rank 1 = score 1.0, rank 8 = score 0.0); B2ₖ = Likert response to item B2 (location as risk proxy), normalized to [0,1]; C2ₖ = scenario response to item C2 (geographic preference scenario), normalized to [0,1]; w₁ = 0.3; w₂ = 0.3; w₃ = 0.4.

*Representativeness Composite Score (RCS):*

$$RCS_k = \frac{w_1 \cdot B1\_rank\_sector_k + w_2 \cdot C3_k}{w_1 + w_2}$$

Where: B1_rank_sectorₖ = normalized inverted rank assigned to a momentum-relevant criterion in B1; C3ₖ = scenario response to C3 (momentum scenario), normalized to [0,1]; w₁ = 0.4; w₂ = 0.6.

*Herding Composite Score (HCS):*

$$HCS_k = \frac{w_1 \cdot B1\_rank\_peer_k + w_2 \cdot B4_k + w_3 \cdot C4_k}{w_1 + w_2 + w_3}$$

Where: B1_rank_peerₖ = normalized inverted rank assigned to "peer PFA activity" in item B1; B4ₖ = Likert response to item B4 (portfolio similarity to peers), normalized to [0,1]; C4ₖ = scenario response to C4 (herding scenario), normalized to [0,1]; w₁ = 0.25; w₂ = 0.35; w₃ = 0.40.

**Population-level prevalence:**
For each heuristic H, prevalence is measured as the mean composite score across all N respondents:

$$\bar{H} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{k=1}^{N} H_k$$

A score of 0.5 represents a neutral midpoint (neither heuristic-consistent nor heuristic-opposed). Scores above 0.5 indicate net heuristic-consistent responses; scores above 0.7 will be classified as "prevalent."

**Analysis:**
Descriptive statistics (mean, standard deviation, minimum, maximum, inter-quartile range) for each composite score across all respondents. Correlation matrix across the four composite scores to test whether heuristics are correlated (co-occurring) or orthogonal (independently distributed). Cross-tabulation by respondent role, fund AUM category, and experience level (using median splits). BCa bootstrap 95% confidence intervals around the mean composite scores (10,000 replications).

**Calibration of heuristic weights (α₁–α₄) for portfolio construction:**
The mean composite scores ₐH̄CS, ĀVCS, R̄CS, H̄CS are normalized to sum to 1 to produce the scoring weights:

$$\alpha_j = \frac{\bar{H}_j}{\sum_{j=1}^{4} \bar{H}_j}$$

These weights (α₁ = ACS weight, α₂ = AVCS weight, α₃ = RCS weight, α₄ = HCS weight) are then used in the heuristic portfolio scoring function in Section 3.5.2. This is the methodological link between Phase 1 (survey) and Phase 2 (portfolio construction) that makes the sequential mixed-methods design coherent.

**Contingency:** If fewer than 15 usable responses are obtained, the composite score calculation remains valid but statistical power is reduced. In this case, descriptive statistics will be presented with wider bootstrap confidence intervals, and cross-tabulation will be limited to two groups (above/below median experience) rather than three.

### 3.5.2 — Financial Metrics Calculation

**Method overview:**
Before either portfolio can be constructed, individual financial metrics must be calculated for each of the 80 properties. These metrics form the inputs to both the heuristic scoring function and the MVO optimizer. All formulae follow standard real estate investment analysis practice as documented in Geltner, Miller, Clayton & Eichholtz (2014) 🟢.

**Formula 3.1 — Total Acquisition Cost (TAC):**

$$TAC_i = P_i \times (1 + f_{agency} + f_{legal} + f_{consent,s_i})$$

Where: Pᵢ = asking price of property i (₦); f_agency = 0.05 (agency fee, 5% of price — standard Nigerian institutional practice); f_legal = 0.05 (legal fee, 5%); f_consent,sᵢ = Governor's Consent fee applicable to state sᵢ (0.10 for Lagos; 0.05 for all other states, reflecting Lagos's higher Governor's Consent rate documented in PenCom regulatory guidance). Note: TAC > Pᵢ for all properties. This is important because optimization is constrained by TAC, not Pᵢ — a property priced at ₦500M costs ₦600M to acquire in Lagos after all transaction costs.

**Formula 3.2 — Net Operating Income (NOI):**

$$NOI_i = (GR_i \times (1 - v_{t_i})) - (GR_i \times m_{a_i})$$

Where: GRᵢ = estimated annual gross rent (₦); v_{t_i} = vacancy rate applicable to property i's asset type tᵢ (0.05 for office, 0.04 for commercial, 0.05 for industrial, 0.06 for residential, 0.06 for mixed-use); m_{a_i} = maintenance rate applicable to asset type aᵢ (0.10 for office, 0.10 for commercial, 0.08 for industrial, 0.15 for residential, 0.12 for mixed-use). Vacancy and maintenance rates are calibrated from CBRE Nigeria (2023) 🟢 and PenCom Annual Report data 🟢.

**Formula 3.3 — Capitalization Rate (Cap Rate):**

$$CR_i = \frac{NOI_i}{TAC_i}$$

The cap rate measures the income yield on total investment cost. It is the fundamental measure of current income return for a buy-and-hold property investment.

**Formula 3.4 — Expected Return:**

$$E[R_i] = CR_i + \mu_{idx(i)}$$

Where: μ_{idx(i)} = mean annual capital appreciation for the market index to which property i is assigned, estimated from the historical return series of index idx(i) (specifically, the arithmetic mean of capital appreciation observations over the documented historical period). This decomposes expected return into its two components: income return (cap rate) and capital return (index appreciation).

**Formula 3.5 — Total Volatility:**

$$\sigma_i = \sigma_{idx(i)} + \delta^{title}_{tl_i} + \delta^{cond}_{cn_i}$$

Where: σ_{idx(i)} = standard deviation of annual total returns for the market index of property i (computed from the index return series); δ^{title}_{tl_i} = title risk premium for title status tl_i (0.00 for C of O; 0.005 for Governor's Consent; 0.030 for Gazette; 0.050 for Excision; 0.020 for Deed of Assignment); δ^{cond}_{cn_i} = condition risk premium for condition cn_i (0.000 for New; 0.005 for Good; 0.015 for Fair; 0.030 for Needs Renovation). These idiosyncratic risk premiums are additive to the systematic index volatility, reflecting the additional volatility arising from property-specific legal and physical risk factors.

**Formula 3.6 — Individual Sharpe Ratio:**

$$SR_i = \frac{E[R_i] - R_f}{\sigma_i}$$

Where: Rf = 0.15 (15% — the average Nigerian T-Bill rate over the 2019–2024 period, computed from CBN Monetary Policy Rate data 🟢). The 15% figure is used rather than the current spot rate (~27.5% as at Q1 2026) because the study's five-year backtest horizon spans 2020–2024, during which the average T-bill rate was approximately 9–15%. Using the backtest-period average rate produces Sharpe ratios that are interpretable within the study's analytical frame. Sensitivity analysis with Rf = 0.10, 0.15, and 0.20 will be presented to document the impact of this parameter choice on findings.

### 3.5.3 — Covariance Matrix Construction

**Method overview:**
The covariance matrix Σ is an N×N matrix (where N = 80) capturing the co-movement of returns between all pairs of properties. It is the risk input to the MVO optimizer. Because property-level return histories are unavailable, Σ is constructed from market index return series using a factor model approach.

**Formula 3.7 — Covariance matrix element (off-diagonal):**

$$\Sigma_{ij} = \rho_{idx(i),idx(j)} \times \sigma_i \times \sigma_j \quad \text{for } i \neq j$$

Where: ρ_{idx(i),idx(j)} = the sample correlation between the annual return series of the market indices to which properties i and j are assigned; σᵢ, σⱼ = total volatilities of properties i and j respectively (from Formula 3.5). This maps property-level covariance to the documented correlation structure of their respective market segments.

**Formula 3.8 — Covariance matrix element (diagonal):**

$$\Sigma_{ii} = \sigma_i^2$$

The diagonal elements are the individual property variances.

**Positive semi-definiteness requirement:**
For MVO to be mathematically valid, Σ must be positive semi-definite (PSD) — all eigenvalues must be non-negative. This will be verified after construction using: min(eigenvalues(Σ)) ≥ −1×10⁻¹⁰ (a numerical tolerance for floating-point rounding). If any eigenvalues are negative, Ledoit-Wolf shrinkage (Ledoit & Wolf, 2004 ⭐🟡 — *A well-conditioned estimator for large-dimensional covariance matrices*, Journal of Multivariate Analysis — verify on ScienceDirect) will be applied to produce a valid, well-conditioned estimate. Ledoit-Wolf shrinkage pulls the sample covariance matrix toward a structured target (a scalar multiple of the identity matrix), eliminating negative eigenvalues while preserving the overall correlation structure.

### 3.5.4 — Portfolio Construction (Objectives I and II)

#### Portfolio A — Heuristic-Driven Portfolio

**Method overview:**
Properties will be selected from the 80-property universe using a priority-ordered scoring algorithm that encodes the four heuristics, with weights calibrated from the questionnaire findings. The algorithm has two stages: (1) hard filters that unconditionally exclude non-compliant properties; (2) a composite scoring function that ranks the remaining properties. Properties are then selected greedily in descending score order until the budget or PenCom concentration limits are exhausted.

**Stage 1 — Hard Filters (applied sequentially in order of priority):**

| Filter | Rule | Heuristic |
|---|---|---|
| F1: PENCOM Compliance | Exclude if commercial lease term < 7 years | Regulatory (not heuristic) |
| F2: Title Quality | Exclude if title ∉ {C of O, Governor's Consent} | Anchoring / risk aversion |
| F3: Location Prestige | Exclude if submarket ∉ defined prime list | Availability |
| F4: Condition | Exclude if condition = Needs Renovation | Condition preference |

The prime submarket list for Filter F3 is derived from questionnaire Section B responses: the top-ranked micro-locations by frequency of mention in Section D open responses will define the prime set, supplemented by standard market classifications (Ikoyi, Victoria Island, Lekki Phase 1 in Lagos; Maitama, Asokoro, Wuse II in Abuja).

**Stage 2 — Composite Heuristic Scoring Function:**

$$H(p_i) = \alpha_1 \cdot TS(p_i) + \alpha_2 \cdot LS(p_i) + \alpha_3 \cdot MS(p_i) + \alpha_4 \cdot PS(p_i)$$

Where:
- H(pᵢ) = composite heuristic score for property i ∈ [0, 1]
- TS(pᵢ) = Title Score: 1.0 if C of O; 0.5 if Governor's Consent; 0.0 otherwise (anchoring dimension)
- LS(pᵢ) = Location Score: normalized prestige score based on submarket tier (Prime = 1.0; Secondary = 0.6; Emerging = 0.2) (availability dimension)
- MS(pᵢ) = Momentum Score: normalized recent return of property i's market index over the prior 3 quarters (representativeness dimension), defined as: MS(pᵢ) = (R_{idx(i),recent} − R_min) / (R_max − R_min)
- PS(pᵢ) = Peer Score: 1.0 if property i is in a submarket where ≥2 peer PFAs have reported holdings in the most recent PenCom quarterly data; 0.0 otherwise (herding dimension)
- α₁, α₂, α₃, α₄ = calibration weights from Section 3.5.1 formula (normalized survey composite scores), where α₁ + α₂ + α₃ + α₄ = 1

**Stage 3 — Greedy Allocation:**
Properties passing all hard filters are sorted by H(pᵢ) in descending order. Starting from the highest-scored property, each property is allocated until one of three stopping conditions is met: (a) budget is exhausted; (b) the property's TAC exceeds the remaining budget; or (c) the property's TAC exceeds 5% of total fund value (PenCom concentration cap). Equal weighting within selected properties: wᵢ = 1 / count(selected).

#### Portfolio B — MVO-Optimized Portfolio

**Method overview:**
Portfolio B will be constructed by solving a binary integer program (BIP) that maximizes the portfolio Sharpe ratio subject to PenCom regulatory constraints and a cardinality constraint. The optimization is implemented in Python using the PuLP library with the CBC (Coin-or Branch and Cut) solver.

**Formula 3.9 — The Binary Integer Program:**

$$\text{Maximize} \quad SR_P = \frac{E[R_P] - R_f}{\sigma_P}$$

Subject to:

$$x_i \in \{0, 1\} \quad \forall i = 1, \ldots, 80 \quad \text{(binary selection)}$$

$$\sum_{i=1}^{80} TAC_i \cdot x_i \leq B \quad \text{(budget constraint)}$$

$$TAC_i \cdot x_i \leq 0.05 \cdot B \quad \forall i \quad \text{(PenCom: max 5\% in any single property)}$$

$$\sum_{i \in S_j} x_i \geq 1 \quad \forall j \in \{1, 2\} \quad \text{(PenCom: min 2 states)}$$

$$\sum_{i \in \text{commercial}} x_i \cdot \mathbf{1}[LT_i < 7] = 0 \quad \text{(PenCom: commercial lease ≥7 years)}$$

$$\sum_{i=1}^{80} x_i \leq K \quad \text{(cardinality: max K properties)}$$

$$\sum_{i=1}^{80} x_i \geq 5 \quad \text{(minimum diversification)}$$

Where: xᵢ = binary selection variable for property i (1 = selected, 0 = not selected); B = total fund value (₦10 billion — a representative mid-sized PFA portfolio); TAC_i = total acquisition cost of property i (Formula 3.1); S_j = set of properties in state j; LTᵢ = lease term of property i in years; K = maximum portfolio size (K = 20, reflecting the binding effect of the 5% concentration cap on a fund of this size); 5 = minimum portfolio size (enforcing at minimum a 5-property portfolio to prevent degenerate single-property solutions).

**Portfolio expected return and volatility:**

$$E[R_P] = \sum_{i=1}^{80} w_i x_i E[R_i] \quad \text{where} \quad w_i = \frac{1}{\sum_{j=1}^{80} x_j}$$

$$\sigma_P = \sqrt{\mathbf{x}^T \boldsymbol{\Sigma} \mathbf{x}} \quad \text{where} \quad \mathbf{x} \text{ is the vector of } x_i w_i$$

**Solver and convergence:**
The CBC solver will be given a time limit of 300 seconds. If the optimal solution is not found within this limit, the best feasible solution found will be accepted and documented as an approximate optimal. If the problem proves infeasible with K = 20, K will be relaxed to 25 and the run repeated. All solver decisions (optimal vs. approximate, K value used) will be documented in the analysis.

### 3.5.5 — Efficient Frontier Analysis (Objective II)

**Method overview:**
The efficient frontier will be estimated empirically by generating 1,000 random feasible portfolios from the 80-property universe and identifying the upper-left boundary of the resulting mean-variance cloud.

**Procedure:**
1. Generate 1,000 random binary portfolios by randomly selecting between 5 and 20 properties from the universe (ensuring all PenCom constraints are satisfied).
2. Compute E[Rₚ] and σₚ for each portfolio using Formulae 3.9 components.
3. Plot the 1,000 portfolios in σ vs. E[R] space.
4. Identify the efficient frontier as the boundary that maximizes E[Rₚ] for each level of σₚ.
5. Plot Portfolio A (heuristic) and Portfolio B (optimized) on the same graph.
6. Compute the Sharpe ratio gap: ΔSR = SR_B − SR_A.
7. Compute Portfolio A's efficiency score: eff_A = SR_A / SR_B × 100% (the percentage of the optimal Sharpe ratio achieved by the heuristic portfolio).

This will produce Figure 4.X in Chapter Four: the efficient frontier scatter plot with both portfolio positions marked.

### 3.5.6 — Monte Carlo Simulation (Objective III)

**Method overview:**
Returns for both portfolios will be simulated over a 60-month (5-year) horizon using Geometric Brownian Motion, producing 10,000 simulation paths per portfolio. The GBM model is the standard continuous-time model for asset price dynamics (Hull, 2018 ⭐🟡 — *Options, Futures, and Other Derivatives*, Pearson, any recent edition — verify) and is appropriate for annual return simulation of real estate indices where returns are approximately continuously compounded.

**Formula 3.10 — GBM monthly return for property i:**

$$R_i(t) = \mu_i \Delta t + \sigma_i \varepsilon_i \sqrt{\Delta t}$$

Where: R_i(t) = realized return of property i in month t; μᵢ = annual drift (= E[Rᵢ] from Formula 3.4); σᵢ = annual volatility (from Formula 3.5); Δt = 1/12 (monthly time step); εᵢ ~ N(0,1) = standard normal random variable. The ε values are correlated across properties according to the correlation structure embedded in Σ, using Cholesky decomposition (see below).

**Cholesky decomposition for correlated shocks:**
To generate correlated returns across properties, the covariance matrix is decomposed as Σ = LL^T where L is the lower Cholesky factor. For each month t in each simulation s:

$$\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(t) = \mathbf{L} \cdot \mathbf{z}(t)$$

Where: **z**(t) is an 80-dimensional vector of independent standard normal draws; **ε**(t) is the correlated shock vector with covariance structure Σ. This ensures that properties assigned to the same market index move together (as they should in reality) while properties in different markets and asset classes exhibit lower correlation.

**Portfolio return aggregation:**

$$R_P(t) = \sum_{i=1}^{80} w_i x_i R_i(t)$$

**Portfolio value path:**

$$V_P(t) = V_P(0) \times \prod_{\tau=1}^{t} (1 + R_P(\tau))$$

Where V_P(0) = 1.0 (normalized initial value). The 60-month portfolio value path is recorded for each simulation. The terminal value V_P(60) is the outcome of interest.

**Simulation parameters:** N_sim = 10,000 paths; T = 60 months; Seed = 42 for base case (same seed as universe generator — ensuring the simulation is embedded in the same reproducible environment as the property universe). Ten additional seed values (43–52) will be used for sensitivity analysis to confirm that findings are not seed-specific.

### 3.5.7 — Performance Metrics Calculation (Objective III)

**Method overview:**
Six performance metrics will be computed for each simulation path and then summarized as distributions across the 10,000 paths. The metrics collectively capture return, risk, and diversification quality.

**Table 3.4: Performance Metrics — Formulae and Operationalization**

| # | Metric | Formula | Variables Defined |
|---|---|---|---|
| M1 | Cumulative Return | $CR = \frac{V_P(60)}{V_P(0)} - 1$ | V_P(0) = 1.0 (normalized); V_P(60) = terminal value |
| M2 | Annualized Return (CAGR) | $CAGR = (1 + CR)^{1/5} - 1$ | T = 5 years |
| M3 | Annualized Volatility | $\sigma_P^{ann} = \text{std}(\{R_P(t)\}_{t=1}^{60}) \times \sqrt{12}$ | Monthly returns annualized by √12 |
| M4 | Sharpe Ratio | $SR_P = \frac{CAGR - R_f}{\sigma_P^{ann}}$ | R_f = 0.15 |
| M5 | Maximum Drawdown | $MDD = \max_{0 \leq t \leq 60} \frac{V_{peak}(t) - V_P(t)}{V_{peak}(t)}$ | V_peak(t) = running maximum of portfolio value up to month t |
| M6 | Diversification Ratio | $DR = \frac{\sum_{i} w_i x_i \sigma_i}{\sigma_P^{ann}}$ | Weighted avg individual vol / portfolio vol; higher = better diversification |

For M4: sensitivity analysis with R_f = 0.10, 0.15, and 0.20 will be run and reported in tabular form. The base case R_f = 0.15 is pre-registered as the primary result.

For M5: A higher MDD indicates greater drawdown vulnerability. The comparison MDD_A vs. MDD_B directly tests whether heuristic portfolios expose contributors to greater peak-to-trough loss risk.

### 3.5.8 — Statistical Significance Testing (Objective III)

**Method overview:**
To determine whether observed performance differences between the two portfolios are statistically significant (rather than noise arising from simulation variance), four statistical procedures will be applied.

**Test 1 — Primary hypothesis test (paired t-test on Sharpe ratio differential):**

Null hypothesis: H₀: E[SR_B] − E[SR_A] = 0
Alternative hypothesis: H₁: E[SR_B] − E[SR_A] > 0

Test statistic:

$$t = \frac{\bar{d}}{\frac{s_d}{\sqrt{N_{sim}}}}$$

Where: d_s = SR_B,s − SR_A,s = difference in Sharpe ratios in simulation s; $\bar{d}$ = mean difference across N_sim = 10,000 simulations; s_d = standard deviation of the differences.

The one-sided test is appropriate because the study's null hypothesis is directional: if optimization has value, it should produce a *higher* (not merely different) Sharpe ratio. A two-sided test would be appropriate only if the study had no prior expectation about direction — but the literature (Section 2.7.3) provides a directional prior.

**Test 2 — Effect size (Cohen's d):**

$$d = \frac{\bar{d}}{s_d}$$

Interpretation thresholds: |d| < 0.2 (negligible); 0.2–0.5 (small); 0.5–0.8 (medium); > 0.8 (large). The effect size is independent of sample size and captures whether the difference is practically meaningful, not just statistically detectable.

**Pre-registered practical significance threshold:**
Beyond statistical significance, this study pre-registers a practical significance threshold of **ΔSR = 0.05 Sharpe units**. A Sharpe ratio difference smaller than 0.05 is considered practically negligible even if statistically significant (given N = 10,000, very small differences will be statistically detectable). This threshold is justified as follows: on a ₦10B fund, a 0.05 Sharpe unit improvement over five years translates to approximately ₦75M–₦100M in risk-adjusted value — a meaningful but not transformative figure for a fund of this scale. If ΔSR ≥ 0.05, the finding is declared both statistically and practically significant.

**Test 3 — Bootstrap Confidence Intervals (BCa):**
Given that real estate returns are not normally distributed (they exhibit skewness and fat tails), asymptotic t-distribution confidence intervals may be unreliable. Bias-corrected and accelerated (BCa) bootstrap confidence intervals are used as a non-parametric alternative. Parameters: block length = 6 months (to preserve the autocorrelation structure of monthly returns); replications = 10,000. The 95% BCa confidence interval for ΔSR will be reported alongside the t-test result.

*Cite:* Efron & Tibshirani (1993) ⭐🟡 — *An Introduction to the Bootstrap* (Chapman & Hall) — for the BCa method. This is a highly cited, real text; verify on WorldCat.

**Test 4 — Market condition sub-analysis:**
The 10,000 simulation paths will be partitioned into terciles based on simulated aggregate market volatility (defined as the cross-sectional standard deviation of property returns in each 12-month period). The three terciles represent low-volatility, medium-volatility, and high-volatility market scenarios. The Sharpe ratio differential ΔSR will be computed separately for each tercile. This tests the ecological rationality prediction that heuristics perform relatively better in high-volatility (high-uncertainty) scenarios — if true, the performance gap should narrow in the high-volatility tercile.

### 3.5.9 — Analysis of Factors Driving Heuristic Use (Objective IV)

**Method overview:**
Questionnaire Section D open-ended and semi-structured responses will be analysed using content analysis and frequency analysis.

**Content analysis procedure:**
A pre-defined coding frame will be applied to all open-text responses. The coding frame is derived from the theoretical framework in Chapter Two (Section 2.6) and includes four first-level categories:

| Code | Category | Theoretical Source |
|---|---|---|
| COG | Cognitive constraints (limited analytical capacity, time pressure) | Simon (1955, 1956) 🟢 |
| INF | Informational constraints (data scarcity, opacity) | Akerlof (1970) 🟢 |
| INS | Institutional / governance constraints (committee structures, career risk) | Scharfstein & Stein (1990) 🟢; Clark & Urwin (2008) 🟢 |
| REG | Regulatory constraints (PenCom rules perceived as substituting for analysis) | PenCom Regulations (2019, 2025) 🟢 |

Both researchers will independently code all responses, with disagreements resolved by discussion. Inter-rater reliability will be measured using Cohen's κ (kappa) — a value of κ ≥ 0.70 will be considered adequate agreement (Landis & Koch, 1977 ⭐🟡 — *The measurement of observer agreement for categorical data*, Biometrics — verify, but this is a real and widely cited paper). If only one researcher is available (as in a single-author dissertation), all responses will be coded twice with a minimum two-week gap between passes to reduce recency bias.

**Frequency analysis:**
For semi-structured items, the percentage of respondents citing each constraint category as significant or very significant (ratings of 4 or 5 on a 5-point scale) will be calculated.

**Cross-tabulation:**
The distribution of constraint categories will be cross-tabulated against respondent heuristic composite scores from Section 3.5.1. Specifically: do respondents with higher composite heuristic scores (more heuristic-consistent) disproportionately cite informational constraints (INF)? This would support the ecological rationality interpretation; heuristics are used because formal analysis is genuinely constrained by data unavailability.

## SECTION 3.6 — Study Limitations

(Note: This section is standard in a methodology chapter and should follow Section 3.5. Your supervisor's structure specifies five main sections; this is an appropriate addition that most methodology chapters include.)

### What to Write

Four paragraphs, each addressing a specific limitation honestly and explaining what mitigations are in place.

**Limitation 1 — Synthetic universe reliance:**
The property universe is synthetically generated rather than drawn from observed Nigerian transactions. This means the study operates in a controlled environment that may not fully replicate the actual distribution of available properties that PFAs encounter. The mitigation is rigorous calibration from documented market sources and formal validation against nine statistical criteria. This approach is consistent with established practice in computational finance (Glasserman, 2004 ⭐🟡) and is methodologically preferable — for this study's specific research question — to a mixed real/synthetic approach that would introduce epistemological inconsistency.

**Limitation 2 — Sample size and representativeness:**
The questionnaire targets 20–25 respondents from 8–10 PFAs — a purposive expert sample that may not be fully representative of all 23 licensed PFAs. Smaller PFAs with no active real estate program are excluded, as are PFAs whose investment staff decline to participate. Bootstrap resampling addresses the statistical power implications of the small N; the representativeness limitation is acknowledged but not fully solvable without a compulsory census.

**Limitation 3 — Risk-free rate assumption:**
The Sharpe ratio calculation uses R_f = 0.15, representing the approximate average Nigerian T-bill rate over the 2019–2024 period. This differs from the current spot rate (~27.5% as at Q1 2026). Using the current spot rate would produce negative Sharpe ratios for most real estate portfolios, which would make the comparison uninformative. The sensitivity analysis (R_f = 0.10, 0.15, 0.20) mitigates this limitation by demonstrating the robustness of findings across a range of risk-free rate assumptions.

**Limitation 4 — Static portfolio assumption:**
The study models buy-and-hold portfolios with no rebalancing over the five-year simulation horizon. Real PFAs may adjust their portfolios over time. Excluding rebalancing understates the potential performance benefit of optimization (which could be re-optimized as market conditions change) and overstates the cost of heuristic path dependency (heuristic portfolios cannot be easily adjusted either, given illiquidity). For a comparative study of selection decision processes, the static assumption is appropriate — it isolates the selection effect from the ongoing management effect.

Every section and sub-section that describes a method must include a sentence of the form: *"This method will address Objective [X] / Research Question [Y]."* This may feel repetitive but is not — your supervisor specifically requires it, and it serves a real function: it makes the chain from question → method → analysis → finding traceable throughout the dissertation.

- Be numbered sequentially (Formula 3.1, 3.2, etc.)
- Have every variable defined immediately below it

Every major method must have at least one sentence addressing what will happen if it fails, produces insufficient data, or yields unexpected results. This is not pessimism — it is methodological maturity. Examiners who understand research know that plans encounter obstacles; the question is whether you have thought through your response.
